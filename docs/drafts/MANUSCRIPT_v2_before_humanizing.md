**Working title:** *How reliable are radiograph body-part classifiers? A leakage-controlled, cross-dataset benchmark with reliability and label-quality analysis on public data*

---
## Abstract
**Background.** Identifying the anatomical region of a radiograph supports PACS routing, hanging protocols and cohort building, but published accuracies are difficult to compare: splits, thresholds and uncertainty are rarely reported rigorously, and most large studies use private data.
**Methods.** We built a leakage-controlled benchmark on the public UNIFESP body-part dataset (22 classes, 1,738 labelled radiographs from 768 patients): patient-level multilabel-stratified folds, a fixed held-out set (323 images, 156 patients), all choices (head/TTA variant, ensemble weights, thresholds) made on out-of-fold predictions only, and patient-level bootstrap confidence intervals. We ran ablations (augmentation, loss, backbones, seeds, CNN-Transformer fusion, split type), a frozen foundation-model baseline, zero-shot transfer to MURA and FracAtlas through an explicit class map, a MURA body-part benchmark, a reliability analysis (calibration, selective prediction) and an out-of-fold label-error audit.
**Results.** On the held-out set the ensemble reached a sample-mean F1 of 0.919 (95 % CI 0.867-0.960); it was not significantly better than its best single member (+0.0066, p = 0.063), and no ablation configuration differed significantly from the reference (all p > 0.08). A gated CNN-Transformer fusion collapsed onto one branch (CNN weight <= 0.003). A random image-level split over-estimated validation F1 by 0.0144 (patient-level: 0.0004), and tuning thresholds on the scored set added 0.0083. A frozen DINOv2-S encoder with a linear probe reached 0.886 (0.833-0.932). On MURA body-part classification the model reached accuracy 0.9747 (0.9663-0.9818) on the official validation set. Zero-shot transfer through the class map was moderate (0.70-0.88 accuracy). The ensemble was already reasonably calibrated (ECE 0.054); accuracy of the retained images rose from 0.926 to 0.945 at 90 % coverage. A label audit flagged 39 of 1,415 training images (2.8 %) as suspected label errors; these are suspects, not confirmed errors. A late Kaggle submission scored 0.9264 on the private leaderboard, between the 2nd (0.9279) and 3rd (0.9249) place.
**Conclusion.** On this public task, careful evaluation matters more than architecture: the reported gains of fusion, ensembling and pseudo-labelling are within the noise of a 323-image held-out set. We release code, splits and seeds.
**Keywords:** body-part classification; radiograph; data leakage; reproducibility; calibration; label noise.

## 1 Introduction
Radiographs enter hospital archives with the anatomical region encoded in a DICOM tag or a free-text study description, and these fields are often wrong or missing: an early audit of 562 images found false *Body Part Examined* entries in 15.3 % (Güld et al., SPIE 2002). Deep learning can recover the region from pixels (Pham et al. 2021; Langer et al. 2024; Mitsuyama et al. 2025) **[VERIFY Pham and Langer numbers; Mitsuyama read in full text]**, yet most large studies use private data, report accuracy only and say little about how splits and thresholds were chosen.
On the public UNIFESP body-part dataset (Farina, Abdala & Kitamura) we found one academic paper, reporting 86.94 % accuracy for the best of five CNNs (Krishnan & Padmavathi 2025) **[VERIFY split and metric]**, and a Kaggle competition whose final private leaderboard is led by a score of 0.93378. Known evaluation shortcuts - leakage across images of the same patient, and thresholds or checkpoints chosen on the scored data (Kapoor & Narayanan 2023; Roberts et al. 2021) - can inflate such numbers, but their size on body-part classification has not, to our knowledge, been measured **[VERIFY on Google Scholar before keeping "to our knowledge"]**.
We therefore ask a methodological question rather than chase a leaderboard: *how much of the reported performance survives a leakage-controlled protocol, and what does a clinical router need beyond accuracy?* Our contributions, each backed by a finished experiment:
1. a reproducible, patient-level benchmark on public data with confidence intervals (Tables 1, 2);
2. a measurement of how much common shortcuts inflate scores (Tables 4, 5);
3. negative results: no ablation, no fusion variant and no ensembling step is significantly better; a gated CNN-Transformer fusion collapses onto one branch (Tables 4, 6);
4. cross-dataset results on MURA and FracAtlas, including a body-part benchmark on the official MURA split (Tables 7-9);
5. a reliability analysis (calibration, risk-coverage) and an out-of-fold label-error audit for routing use (Section 5.7);
6. a frozen foundation-model baseline with a label-efficiency curve (Section 5.6) and a Grad-CAM faithfulness audit (Table 10).

## 2 Related work
**Body-part classification.** Pham et al. (2021) classify five coarse groups on 16,093 images for DICOM routing (recall 0.982) and note that routing needs near-perfect accuracy. Langer et al. (2024, preprint) classify 14 regions on 48,434 hospital radiographs (96.6 % single model, 97.7 % ensemble) and show by expert review that 35 % of test-set errors are wrong labels and 11 % out-of-domain images, which suggests that headline accuracy is partly limited by label noise. Mitsuyama et al. (Eur Radiol 2025; 2026;36:2576-2590) trained a seven-category model (Head, Neck, Chest, Incomplete Chest, Abdomen, Pelvis, Extremities) on 429,341 radiographs from two institutions and MURA and tested it externally on a second institution and on MURA (all MURA images labelled 'Extremities'; micro-accuracy 98.6 % on MURA, 99 % CI 98.4-98.7 %). Radiologist review changed the DICOM-tag-derived body-part label of 3.9 % of the radiographs of one institution and 12.8 % of the other. That MURA figure is not comparable with ours: it is a seven-way coarse task in which every MURA image belongs to one class, whereas we separate the seven MURA study types. On MURA, Shah et al. (2026) report body-part accuracy 0.9643 (macro-F1 0.9574) on MURA with an attention-fused Xception-Swin model **[VERIFY: split, seeds and whether CIs are reported; full text not accessible]**. These studies use different data, class sets and metrics and cannot be compared directly with ours.
**Hybrid CNN-Transformer models** with gated or attention fusion are common; we did not find a report that inspects the learned gate **[VERIFY: absence is not proof]**. **Hierarchical losses:** Wu, Tygert & LeCun (2019) showed that directly minimising a hierarchical loss is hard and that minimising cross-entropy often lowers the hierarchical loss almost as much, suggesting it is more useful as a metric than as a training objective.
**Evaluation quality.** Leakage is widespread in ML-based science (Kapoor & Narayanan 2023) and in COVID-19 imaging (Roberts et al. 2021); dataset label quality limits medical-imaging benchmarks (Oakden-Rayner 2020); reporting guidance exists (CLAIM 2024 update, Tejani et al.). Confident-learning methods find label errors from out-of-fold probabilities (Northcutt et al. 2021) **[VERIFY: add reference]**.

## 3 Data
**UNIFESP** (Farina, Abdala & Kitamura, Kaggle; CC BY-NC-SA 4.0): 2,481 DICOM radiographs (de-identified, obtained from the Universidade Federal de São Paulo under IRB approval; the Kaggle version is reduced to 256 x 256 pixels), 20 body regions plus "Others", multilabel annotation by radiology residents on the MD.ai platform (four residents are named in the dataset's acknowledgements; the number of readers per image, adjudication and patient demographics are not stated). 1,738 training images from 768 patients (63 % of patients have several images) and 743 test images from 330 patients with hidden labels; no patient appears in both. All images are 256 x 256; 95 % are MONOCHROME1 (inverted). The DICOM *BodyPartExamined* tag is empty in all 2,481 files, so the tag-error rate cannot be measured here. 92.4 % of training images carry a single label (122 two labels, 10 three); class frequency ranges from 738 (Chest) to 9 (Clavicles). A metadata-only random forest (photometric interpretation, bits stored, size; patient-grouped CV) reached accuracy 0.486 vs 0.451 for the majority class (macro-F1 0.059): acquisition metadata carry almost no label information.
**Splits.** Patient-level multilabel-stratified (iterative stratification, seed 42): a fixed held-out set of 323 images / 156 patients and a CV pool of 1,415 images / 612 patients in five folds. The held-out set has 1-6 positives for Clavicles (1), Skull (2), Forearm (3), Elbow (3) and Thigh (3), so per-class numbers for these classes are unstable.
**MURA** (Rajpurkar et al. 2018): body part taken from the `XR_*` folder name (7 classes), official train/validation split. **FracAtlas** (Abedeen et al., Sci Data 2023, CC BY 4.0): 4,083 images from three hospitals in Bangladesh annotated by two radiologists and an orthopaedist; the 3,685 images with a single region (hand, hip, leg, shoulder) were used for zero-shot transfer. **COVID-19 Radiography** (Chowdhury et al. 2020): 4 classes; it has no patient identifiers, so its split is image-level.

## 4 Methods
**Pre-processing.** Rescale slope/intercept, inversion of MONOCHROME1 images, per-image min-max normalisation to 8 bit, 3-channel replication, resize to 224 (Swin-T) or 256 pixels, ImageNet mean/std.
**Models.** ImageNet-pretrained timm backbones (Swin-T, DenseNet121, EfficientNetV2-S, ConvNeXt-T, ResNet50, EfficientNet-B3), each with a 22-way sigmoid head and a 22-way softmax head. Fusion ablation: DenseNet121 + Swin-T with a learned gate on raw features, gate with LayerNorm, fixed 0.5/0.5 average, CNN only, Swin only.
**Training.** AdamW (weight decay 1e-4; lr 2e-4 Swin, 3e-4 DenseNet/ResNet/EfficientNet, 1e-4 ConvNeXt), cosine schedule with one warm-up epoch, 30 epochs, batch 32, fp16, EMA of weights (0.998), gradient clipping 5. Loss: BCE-with-logits with positive weights capped at 20 plus 0.5 x soft-target cross-entropy on the softmax head. Augmentation: flip, shift-scale-rotate, brightness/contrast ("light"), plus noise/blur ("heavy"). No checkpoint selection: final EMA weights are used.
**Decisions on out-of-fold (OOF) predictions only:** head/TTA variant per run, ensemble weights (Caruana greedy, 40 iterations), per-class thresholds (coordinate ascent on sample-mean F1, shrunk towards 0.5 with weight n_pos/(n_pos+20)), and an "at least one label" rule. The held-out set is used once.
**Metrics and statistics.** Sample-mean F1 (the competition metric), macro-F1, micro-F1, subset accuracy; 95 % CIs by patient-level bootstrap (2,000 resamples; 1,000 for the reliability analysis); paired patient bootstrap for model comparisons. For FracAtlas and the cross-dataset results the targets have no patient identifiers, so CIs are image-level bootstraps and optimistic.
**Frozen foundation-model baseline.** Frozen encoders (DINOv2-S/B, ImageNet Swin-T) at 224 px, features standardised on the CV pool, a linear 22-sigmoid probe trained with BCE and L2, weight decay chosen on OOF, same folds and held-out set; label-efficiency curve using 10/25/50/100 % of the training patients per fold.
**Cross-dataset transfer.** A closed-set class map between label spaces (e.g. MURA hand/wrist/forearm to the UNIFESP classes of the same name); predictions are restricted to the shared groups and accuracy/macro-F1 reported. The map is an assumption.
**Reliability analysis.** Ensemble of five saved ablation runs (three Swin-T seeds, DenseNet121, ConvNeXt-T; weights fitted on OOF). Top-1 calibration (ECE, 15-bin; temperature fitted on OOF, applied to the held-out set), risk-coverage curve (abstain on low confidence), and per-class precision/recall/F1 with patient bootstrap. **Label audit:** a training image is a *suspect* when the OOF ensemble assigns >= 0.9 probability to a class other than its given label(s) and a low probability (confident-learning style); suspects are not proven errors.
**Explainability.** Grad-CAM for three fold-0 models on the held-out set; a deletion test against a random map of the same grid and a randomisation sanity check.
**Hardware/software [FILL]:** Kaggle notebooks, 2 x NVIDIA T4 (Stage 2: 18,811 s), PyTorch 2.11, library versions from run logs; code, split files and seeds to be released (GitHub + Zenodo DOI).

## 5 Results
### 5.1 Main result (UNIFESP held-out set; Table 1)
Ensemble of six runs with thresholds tuned on OOF: sample-mean F1 0.9189 [0.8669, 0.9602], macro-F1 0.8630 [0.6891, 0.8916], micro-F1 0.9148, subset accuracy 0.8885; at threshold 0.5 the values are 0.9164 / 0.8537. The best single run by OOF (ConvNeXt-T) reached 0.9123. Ensemble minus best single: +0.0066 [-0.0002, +0.0142], p = 0.063. Single runs (Table 2) lie between 0.910 and 0.922.
### 5.2 Competition score (Table 3)
Late submissions (not ranked on the leaderboard; **all submissions made are reported**): Stage 1 ensemble private 0.92563 / public 0.92704; Stage 2 16-model ensemble private 0.92638 / public 0.93378. The finished private leaderboard has 0.93378 (1st), 0.92787 (2nd), 0.92488 (3rd), so both late submissions would fall between 2nd and 3rd. The private score uses about 60 % of the 743 test images with the original, partly noisy labels; we estimate its standard error at about 0.012, so the gap to 1st (0.0074) is not resolvable and the gain of Stage 2 over Stage 1 (+0.00075) is not distinguishable from noise.
**Stage 2 (OOF on all 1,738 training images, not comparable to the held-out numbers; Table 11):** plain ensemble (8 models) 0.9262 [0.9092, 0.9414], the same models trained with pseudo-labels from 539 of the 743 unlabelled test images (confidence >= 0.9) 0.9233 [0.9058, 0.9392], union (16 models) 0.9279 [0.9116, 0.9428]; paired patient-bootstrap differences: union minus plain +0.0017 [-0.0010, +0.0046] (p = 0.25), pseudo-labelled minus plain -0.0029 [-0.0092, +0.0034] (p = 0.36). Pseudo-labelling alone did not help (it lowered OOF and macro-F1 0.8749 -> 0.8587); the union gains +0.0017 with weights fitted on the same OOF, which is optimistic. The pseudo-labelled set contains no Hand, Hip, Lumbar Spine, Thoracic Spine, Skull, Thigh, Forearm or Clavicles images and 322 Chest images, so it is skewed to frequent classes. Because the test images are used transductively, this procedure is not reported as a generalisation result.
### 5.3 Evaluation shortcuts (Tables 4, 5)
Training and validating with a random image-level split gave a validation score that exceeded the held-out score by 0.0144, against 0.0004 for the patient-level reference (the held-out score of the leaky model, 0.9163, is not significantly different: p = 0.141, so leakage inflates the *validation estimate*, not the held-out result). Tuning per-class thresholds on the held-out set itself gave 0.9182 versus 0.9099 for thresholds tuned on OOF (+0.0083), i.e. a gain from the test set. Earlier notebooks that tuned thresholds and selected checkpoints on the scored fold reported 0.9356 (sample-F1) and 0.909 (macro-F1), compared with 0.9189 and 0.8630 under the nested protocol.
### 5.4 Ablations (Table 4)
Against the reference Swin-T (0.9089 [0.8576, 0.9509]), no configuration differs significantly: heavy augmentation -0.0075 (p = 0.088), no softmax head -0.0051 (p = 0.152), seeds +0.0055/+0.0065 (p = 0.37/0.27), DenseNet121 +0.0111 (p = 0.119), CNN-only fusion branch +0.0117 (p = 0.097), averaged three seeds +0.0049 (p = 0.38). All 95 % CIs include zero. Seed-to-seed spread (0.9089-0.9154) is as large as most effects, so single-run differences of this size should not be reported as improvements.
### 5.5 Fusion gate (Table 6)
The learned gate assigned the CNN branch a mean weight of 0.0000 (raw gate) and 0.0029 (LayerNorm gate) across five folds: the fused model effectively reduces to the Transformer. Neither gate variant improved on the fixed average (diff +0.0047 and +0.0069 vs reference, p = 0.40 / 0.23) or on the CNN-only branch.
### 5.6 Frozen foundation-model baseline
A linear probe on frozen DINOv2-S reached OOF 0.8699 and held-out 0.8859 [0.8327, 0.9318]; DINOv2-B 0.8839 [0.8300, 0.9276]; ImageNet Swin-T 0.8762 [0.8248, 0.9204]; the fine-tuned Swin-T reference reached 0.9089. Frozen features thus recover most, but not all, of the fine-tuned performance. With 10 % of the training patients the probe reached OOF 0.6435 (DINOv2-S), 25 % 0.7457, 50 % 0.8235, 100 % 0.8699: performance is still rising with data, which suggests the task is data-limited. *Note:* a random-weight ResNet18 control gave OOF 0.563 but held-out 0.095, an inconsistency we did not resolve; it is excluded from the claims. BiomedCLIP could not be downloaded and a ConvNeXt tag was invalid; both were skipped.
### 5.7 Reliability and label audit (Section tables from `results/reliability/`)
*Calibration.* Top-1 accuracy of the five-model ensemble on the held-out set was 0.9257; ECE 0.0545, and 0.0517 after temperature scaling (T = 1.15): the ensemble is already close to calibrated and scaling changes little. *Selective prediction.* Accuracy of retained images rose from 0.926 (full coverage) to 0.945 [0.900, 0.985] at 90 % coverage, 0.973 at 80 % and 0.982 at 70 %. A threshold chosen on OOF for 0.95 accuracy gave 0.937 accuracy at 98.5 % coverage on the held-out set (target missed), while a 0.98 target gave 0.983 accuracy at 74 % coverage: OOF-chosen operating points do not transfer exactly. *Per class (held-out).* Chest F1 0.993 (n = 138); Cervical Spine, Feet, Hand, Sinus, Skull, Thigh 1.0; weakest: Others recall 0.41 [0.13, 0.91], Forearm 0.33, Hip 0.33, Shoulder 0.60, Ankle 0.75 (n_pos 3-22; wide CIs). *Label audit.* 39 of 1,415 training images (2.8 %) were flagged at p >= 0.9; the most frequent pairs were Hip -> Pelvis (5), Wrist -> Ankle (4), Others -> Chest (3). The Hip -> Pelvis pattern parallels the low Hip recall, consistent with either label noise or a genuine class boundary problem. *Error analysis (Table 12).* Across the three explained fold-0 models the most frequent held-out misses were Others -> Abdomen (6-8 images per model) and Others -> Pelvis (4-5), followed by Hip -> Pelvis (2 per model) and Forearm -> Lower Leg (2 per model): the heterogeneous 'Others' class is mostly absorbed by large-body-region classes. The 39 label-audit suspects are **suspects**; none has been reviewed by a clinician.
### 5.8 Generalisation (Tables 7-9)
*MURA body-part (official split).* 5-fold bagged ensemble: accuracy 0.9747 [0.9663, 0.9818], macro-F1 0.9707 [0.9616, 0.9788]; Shah et al. (2026) report 0.9643 / 0.9574 (**[VERIFY: split, seeds, CIs]**). *COVID-19 Radiography (4 classes, image-level split).* Sample-F1 0.9647 [0.9595, 0.9697], macro-F1 0.9681; numbers may be optimistic. *Zero-shot transfer* (Table 9): MURA -> UNIFESP 0.875-0.880 (n = 184); UNIFESP -> MURA 0.813-0.821 (n = 12,000); MURA -> FracAtlas (hand and shoulder only; 1,281 of 1,382 images are hand) 0.691 (softmax) / 0.858 (sigmoid); UNIFESP -> FracAtlas (hand, hip, leg, shoulder; n = 3,685) 0.708 / 0.698. Many FracAtlas JPEGs are truncated and were decoded partially. Transfer is moderate and head-dependent; the class maps are assumptions.
### 5.9 Explainability (Table 10)
For all three models, deleting the most salient pixels first removed class evidence faster than deleting a random block of the same granularity (gain 0.284 [0.252, 0.316] ConvNeXt-T, 0.204 ResNet50, 0.164 Swin-T), and CAMs were uncorrelated with those of randomly initialised networks (rho 0.11 and 0.02), except ResNet50 (rho -0.31, i.e. different from, but systematically negatively related to, the random-network map). The grids are coarse (7 x 7 to 8 x 8), there is no localisation ground truth and no radiologist review; maps are therefore reported as faithful to the model, not as clinically valid.

## 6 Discussion
**Evaluation matters more than architecture.** Ensembling, fusion, heavy augmentation, extra seeds and pseudo-labelling all moved the held-out score by at most about 0.01, and none significantly; seed noise is of the same size. Differences of this order reported without CIs, as is common, should be read with caution.
**Why the ceiling.** Errors concentrate in rare classes (1-6 held-out positives) and the heterogeneous "Others" class; the audit's Hip -> Pelvis cluster and the competition hosts' offline re-annotation of the test set point to label noise as part of the ceiling, as Langer et al. (2024) found on a hospital dataset. The label-efficiency curve is still rising, so more labelled data would likely help more than architectural changes.
**Gate collapse.** A possible explanation (not tested here) is that a gate learned on raw features of two networks with different activation scales lets one branch dominate; the learned weights indicate a degenerate solution rather than complementary fusion. Fixed averaging was no worse.
**Clinical use.** At 90 % coverage a router retains 94.5 % top-1 accuracy and sends 10 % of images to a human; the OOF-chosen operating point should be re-validated on local data. We position the classifier as a flagging tool for suspicious metadata, not as an autonomous replacement.
**Relation to prior work.** Our MURA figure is higher than the single published number we found for this task, but other studies report 97-99 % on other data and with much larger private training sets; we make no claim of superiority.

## 7 Limitations
Single-centre dataset of 1,738 labelled images; 323 held-out images and 1-6 positives for several classes; COVID and FracAtlas results use image-level splits and CIs; class maps for transfer are my own and unreviewed; the label audit and Grad-CAM are not clinician-validated; the private test labels are noisy and the Stage 2 procedure uses unlabelled test images (transductive) and OOF-fitted weights; the explainability analysis uses a single fold per model; results come from several Kaggle accounts (all reproducible from the released code); one control (random-weight probe) was inconsistent and is excluded; no external clinical site.

## 8 Conclusion
On public UNIFESP data a carefully evaluated ensemble reaches sample-F1 0.919 (0.867-0.960) with no significant benefit from fusion, extra seeds or pseudo-labels; shortcuts such as random splits and test-set thresholds inflate estimates; MURA body-part classification reaches about 97 % accuracy on the official validation set; and the model is reasonably calibrated, supports selective prediction, and exposes a small set of suspected label errors for expert review.
